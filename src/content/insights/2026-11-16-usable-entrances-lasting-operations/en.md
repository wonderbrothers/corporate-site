Period covered: November 9–15, 2026

A service does not get used just because it exists. It becomes usable when people can find the way in, when there is support if they get stuck along the way, and when the operation behind it keeps running. This week we widen the view of design beyond the web to public space, resource circulation, and preparing for heat.

The developments covered are at different stages: a survey, a participatory exhibition, agenda items for a regulatory discussion, and a draft international roadmap. What each one shows is room to design not only how things look or what facilities exist, but also how people behave and the operations that support them.

## 1. Design × accessibility: broken entrances show up in basic components

On November 10, Accessible, a company that provides accessibility services, published a survey of 12,682 websites run by small businesses in the US. It ran automated checks on 53,464 pages on November 9–10 and found at least one WCAG A/AA failure on 90.1% of the sites. Insufficient text contrast appeared on 71.5% of sites, and links without a name that screen readers can identify on 48.3%. The sample was drawn from public business listings, not a random sample of all US websites. And passing automated checks is not proof that a site is accessible.

What the numbers point to is that room for improvement remains in basic components such as text and links, more than in specialized features. Pale text or icon-only links may work as decoration, yet for some users they close off the entrance to information or a procedure. This is a survey by the provider itself, and its percentages cannot be applied directly to sites in Japan, but it does give grounds for looking for fixes in shared templates.

A good first step is to choose one key action, such as booking or making an inquiry, and check it from entry to completion with automated tests and with keyboard and screen reader operation. One approach is to record how many pages the same problem spreads to, whether people can complete the task after the fix, and how much time the person in charge needs to check it. Looking at the number of issues detected together with where people actually get stuck shows whether fixing shared components or redesigning individual flows should come first.

Source: [Main Street Accessibility Report — 9 in 10 small-business websites have accessibility failures a free tool can find](https://accessible.so/research/main-street-2026) (Accessible, November 10, 2026)

## 2. Design × public space: turning "please take part" into borrowing and donating

On November 12, Artiql announced that the "Hirameki Public Design Exhibition" would open on November 13 at PLAT UMEKITA in Osaka. Beyond introducing 12 projects from around the world, it combines hands-on material displays, a library of things, and a bookshelf built by visitors. The library of things displays tools, play equipment, and other items, some of which visitors can actually borrow and use inside the venue. This is a participatory exhibition, not the launch of a full lending service for the local area.

What is instructive is a structure that does not let interest in public space end with simply voicing an opinion. When there are concrete actions, such as borrowing, donating, or leaving a message in a book, participants experience being both a user and a supporter. At the same time, reactions to an exhibition are not the same as everyday demand for a service. Even if many people find it interesting, it will not lead to ongoing use if lending hours or return points do not fit their lives.

In the shared space of a facility or workplace, for example, you could run a short lending trial with a few items that are safe to handle, with provisional rules for check-out, hours, returns, and damage. It is worth keeping track not only of how many people borrowed something, but also why others did not, how much effort returns took, and how much time the staff managing it spent. Capturing the enjoyment of taking part alongside the burden of running it makes it easier to judge whether making it permanent is worthwhile, or whether the rules need to change.

Source: ["From someone's things to everyone's place": the "Hirameki Public Design Exhibition" opens at PLAT UMEKITA on November 13](https://artiql.jp/news/plat-umekita-public-design-202611/) (Artiql, November 12, 2026; in Japanese)

## 3. Design × resource circulation: beyond collection lies the design of handover and wind-down

At a workshop on textile regulations that CalRecycle in California announced for November 12, the agenda included not only producer responsibility plans but also contingency plans, plan termination, revocation or suspension of the operating organization, and annual reports. It is a call for comments on regulatory concepts based on the Responsible Textile Recovery Act of 2024, and what we were able to confirm is the official meeting notice and agenda. It does not mean new regulations were finalized that week.

When people think of circular services, they tend to focus on collection boxes and recycled materials. This agenda, however, shows that what happens when the organization supporting the cycle stops functioning is also part of the design. If clothing or items for repair that people have dropped off get stuck along the way, who explains the situation, and who hands them on to the next recipient? This behind-the-scenes division of roles also affects whether people feel confident taking part in returns and collection.

Not only for clothing but for any service that involves collection or repair, it helps to follow a single item from "intake → storage → assessment → next process" and map out who is responsible and what is recorded at each stage. From there, one approach is to run a tabletop exercise that assumes a contractor stops operating or collection capacity is exceeded, and check where items lose track, how long communication takes, and who could take over instead. It offers a way to find operational weak points that collection volume alone does not reveal, before scaling up.

Source: [Public Meeting Notice: Textile Stewardship Informal Regulatory Concepts Workshop](https://www2.calrecycle.ca.gov/PublicNotices/Details/7228) (CalRecycle, announced August 27, 2026; scheduled for November 12, 2026) / [Textile Stewardship](https://calrecycle.ca.gov/epr/textiles/) (CalRecycle, listing the November 12, 2026 agenda)

## 4. Design × heat resilience: from cooling equipment to keeping people and services going

A November 12 draft from the Cool Coalition, led by UNEP, titled "Global Heat Resilience and Sustainable Cooling Roadmap," deals together with protecting people from heat, keeping essential services running, and meeting climate goals. The order it sets out is to start from risk, reduce cooling demand through shading and nature-based, low-energy approaches, and meet the remaining demand with efficient equipment and similar means. It is at the stage of being designed and piloted with countries in 2027 and presented at COP32; participation is voluntary, and the document does not create new obligations.

Applied to facilities and services, this framework brings out situations where "adding more air-conditioned places" is not enough. Even if there is somewhere cool to go, the route there or the outdoor queue may be hot, opening hours may not match when people need it, or information may stop along with the power during an outage. Improving equipment, information, getting there, and opening hours separately may still leave danger or inconvenience partway through people's experience.

To prepare for the next hot season, you could start with one facility, layering waiting times, shade, rest spots, and ways of providing information over the route from arrival to departure, and looking for the sections where people tend to struggle based on past crowding records and staff observations. Next, it is worth trying small changes, such as moving the waiting area into the shade or spreading out reception times, and comparing waiting time, the physical burden of getting around, and the load on staff. Making safety decisions together with facility management and health experts provides material for deciding how to combine investment in equipment with changes to operations.

Source: [Global Heat Resilience and Sustainable Cooling Roadmap: Protecting people, keeping the lights on, delivering on our climate goals in a warming world](https://coolcoalition.org/download/file/e59fcef4-9071-43e9-8fa6-00457243d355?filename=4.+Global+Heat+Resilience+and+Sustainable+Cooling+Roadmap_0.pdf) (UNEP / Cool Coalition, draft dated November 12, 2026)

## Priority actions for this week

1. **Try checking the entrance where people first get stuck, through one action.** Focusing on booking or inquiries on the web, or on the journey from arrival to reception at a facility, helps you find barriers early that are hard to see in department-by-department reviews.
2. **Map out operations after launch, including exceptions and wind-down.** Following one case, whether lending, repair, or collection, through situations where an item cannot be returned or a contractor stops lets you check for gaps in responsibility and handover before expanding.
3. **Try measuring the burden on both users and operators in a small experiment.** Recording why people did not use the service and how much time staff spent, alongside usage numbers and satisfaction, gives you the material to decide whether to continue, change, or stop.
