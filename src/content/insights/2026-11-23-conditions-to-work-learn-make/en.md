Period covered: November 16–22, 2026

When a new tool or service arrives, it will not lead to the results people hoped for unless there is time, skill, permission, and support to use it. This week we turn to the conditions that support use, drawing on research into how work is changing, a new plan for education support, an exhibition about local making, and admin features in a design tool.

What the four developments have in common is that there is something to design beyond the finished screen or product. Who do you share the work with? How do people get back on track when their learning is interrupted? Who do you shape materials with? How is the use of external tools approved? Dealing with systems like these connects better experiences with how an organization actually runs.

## 1. Design × organizations: rethinking the impact of AI through how work is divided and learned

On November 17, the OECD and Eurofound published a research report, "Artificial intelligence and work organisation." Using the Survey of Adult Skills (PIAAC) and the European Working Conditions Survey (EWCS), it analyzes how work changed over roughly 2011–2024 and how that relates to each occupation's exposure to AI. Manual tasks declined while problem solving, information processing, and teamwork increased, and in occupations more exposed to AI, the rise in routine work was comparatively small. The report also suggests that rising levels of education may have played a larger role in some of these changes. It is a study of associations at the level of occupations, not evidence of the effect of adopting AI in individual workplaces or of cause and effect.

In light of these findings, the number of tools adopted alone looks like a weak measure of organizational change. Even if drafts of documents get faster, waiting time across the team will not go down if checking the evidence falls to a single person. When the content of work changes, there is room to revisit who is assigned what, who people can turn to, and the time they have to learn again.

A realistic place to start is to pick one recurring task and record, for a week, the time spent on drafting, checking, deciding, and handing over. Noting years of experience and whether people have had training, and comparing rework and time spent waiting for checks rather than working time alone, gives you clues for separating a lack of tools from a lack of skills or a poor division of work.

Source: [Artificial intelligence and work organisation](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/artificial-intelligence-and-work-organisation_5a208f6b-en.html) (OECD / Eurofound, November 17, 2026)

## 2. Design × education: beyond delivering materials, building a path to keep learning

On November 18, Sony and UNICEF announced a new partnership on education support. Under the plan, Sony will contribute US$4.5 million over 2027–2029 to support flexible funding for education, the expansion of Learning Passport, a learning platform that works both online and offline, and the Creative Academy, which connects young people's creative and digital skills with opportunities for work and entrepreneurship. The scope covers global development of Learning Passport and its rollout in Nigeria, and the launch of the Creative Academy in Nigeria in its first year. The announcement is a plan for future support; no learning outcomes have been reported.

What stands out is that it combines not just the content of learning materials but also the ability to use them when connectivity is unreliable, and the opportunities that follow. If a learning service is designed only around "log in and view the materials," it can overlook people who share a device, people whose connection drops partway through, and people who have no one to try out their new skills with. Access, continuity, skill acquisition, and next opportunities each need to be treated as separate outcomes.

In corporate training or community learning support too, it is worth taking one short piece of material and testing situations such as resuming after a connection drop, or working through it with someone's help. Recording not only completion rates but also time to resume, the number of questions, and the time supporters spend, and then checking whether people can actually solve a task after learning, brings to light barriers to learning that user numbers alone do not show.

Source: [Sony and UNICEF partner on digital learning, creative skills and new opportunities for young people](https://www.unicef.org/partnerships/sony-and-unicef-partner-digital-learning-creative-skills-and-new-opportunities) (UNICEF, November 18, 2026)

## 3. Design × place and environment: thinking about material choices and who takes part in making, together

On November 19, "Future Observatory: A Matter of Land" opened at the Design Museum in London. It is a free exhibition presenting seven design research projects that explore our relationship with land and nature. In one of them, "Sussex S.E.E Chairs," the University of Brighton is working with Making It Out, an organization that supports people leaving prison and people in recovery from addiction. Through work such as prototyping chairs from local waste timber, the research aims to connect a shared making process with social, economic, and environmental goals. The project has been underway for some time and is not something that started this week. Its project page also does not include comparative figures demonstrating reductions in environmental impact or benefits for participants.

The question this example raises is whether environmental care can be complete with material substitution alone. Even with the same local timber, what the system means changes depending on who can take part in sourcing and processing, what skills they learn, and whether selling and maintaining the product supports further work. Designing while keeping these conditions in view together, rather than optimizing the product's form, the manufacturing process, and support for participants separately, seems likely to surface challenges to continuity sooner.

When replacing small furnishings or fixtures, you could start by taking a single item and mapping the flow from obtaining materials through processing, inspection, and repair. It is worth estimating not only waste and cost but also instruction time, which steps people can safely take on, and variation in quality. Comparisons like these help make concrete the conditions under which local sourcing and shared making can work, and the steps where support is needed.

Source: [Future Observatory: A Matter of Land](https://futureobservatory.org/programme/exhibitions/future-observatory-a-matter-of-land) (Future Observatory / Design Museum, opened November 19, 2026) / [Sussex S.E.E Chairs](https://futureobservatory.org/research/strands/design-accelerators/design-accelerators-2025/sussex-s-e-e-chairs) (Future Observatory, no publication date given; checked November 23, 2026)

## 4. Design × operations: moving external tool connections from individual judgment to an approval process

On November 17, Figma announced the ability to manage an allowlist of OAuth apps and MCP clients. Rolling out over several days to Organization and Enterprise plans, it lets admins review connection requests and permissions from tools such as Cursor and Claude Code. According to Figma's help center, the default setting allows all apps. Switching to require admin approval affects existing connections as well as new ones, and unapproved apps lose access to the organization's resources. Even after an app is approved for the organization, each user still needs to authorize the connection to their own account separately.

As the points where information passes from design into implementation and other work multiply, convenient integrations and business continuity need to be handled together. An allowlist is one way to do that, but simply flipping the setting can suddenly cut off integrations people rely on. Which apps are covered, what permissions they need, where to apply, who reviews requests, and what alternatives exist when a request is rejected all shape the experience for users.

Before rolling it out, it is worth listing the integrations currently in use and then, in a test environment with no confidential information, trying three states: approved, unapproved, and approval revoked. Checking not just whether access succeeds but also how long it takes from request to resuming use, and whether the reason for a rejection reaches the user, makes it easier to judge the operating conditions that strengthen protection without bringing design work to a halt.

Source: [Manage an allowlist of approved OAuth apps, including MCP clients](https://www.figma.com/release-notes/) (Figma, November 17, 2026) / [Manage OAuth apps and MCP clients for your organization](https://help.figma.com/hc/en-us/articles/44716871463319-Manage-OAuth-apps-and-MCP-clients-for-your-organization) (Figma Help Center, no update date given; checked November 23, 2026)

## Priority actions for this week

1. **Try separating working time from time spent waiting for checks in one task.** To avoid aiming improvements at the wrong place, a realistic first step is to record drafting, checking, deciding, and handing over for a week. You may find problems that can be solved through how work is divided or how learning is supported, before adding any tools.
2. **Try out one situation where a service "stops partway."** It is worth choosing a moment that falls outside the usual path to completion, such as a dropped connection during learning or waiting for approval of an external tool. Measuring time to recover and the burden on supporters shows the conditions under which people can keep using it.
3. **Try mapping the process from material to repair for one small item.** Starting with a single piece of furniture and setting cost, waste, instruction load, and quality side by side lets you compare realistic options for balancing environmental care with opportunities to take part.
